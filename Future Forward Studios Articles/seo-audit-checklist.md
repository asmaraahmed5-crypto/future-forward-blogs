# SEO Audit Checklist: How to Audit Your Own Site

SEO audits help you find problems that stop people or search engines from accessing, understanding, and using your website. Start with important pages, verify each finding, and turn the results into a short action list rather than trying to fix every automated warning at once.

## What an SEO audit covers — SEO audits with a clear scope

### Define what you are reviewing

A useful audit covers technical access, page presentation, content usefulness, and the visitor’s next step. These areas connect, but one tool rarely answers every question.

Write down the website’s purpose and the pages that support it. For a service business, include the homepage, main service page, About page, and contact journey.

For a publication, add an article, a topic page, and the route readers use to find related guides. For a store, include representative product and purchase journeys.

### Start with representative pages

Group pages by template or function before reviewing a large archive. A shared navigation problem may affect many URLs, while an unclear paragraph may belong to one article.

Sampling is a starting point, not proof that every unreviewed page is healthy. Expand the check when you find a pattern or an important unresolved question.

Keep the scope in the report. Another person should understand which pages were reviewed and which areas still need investigation.

### Choose tools for specific questions

Use a browser for the visible journey, Search Console for questions about your site’s search presence, and an appropriate diagnostic or crawler for the issue being investigated.

The best site audit tools for your process are the ones that provide evidence you understand and can act on. A large report without clear decisions does not establish that the audit is complete.

## Technical checks

### Check public access

- [ ] Open important public pages while logged out.
- [ ] Confirm that the intended content appears.
- [ ] Follow the main navigation and relevant internal links.
- [ ] Record unavailable pages and unexpected destinations.

Give each finding an exact URL and a description of what happened. “The Contact link opens an unavailable page” is more useful than “The site has technical problems.”

Confirm that a reported failure is repeatable before assuming the cause. Access settings, a publishing mistake, and a server problem require different investigations.

### Review indexing intent

Decide which pages should be public search destinations and which should not. Private account areas, previews, and unfinished pages need different treatment from published guides.

Search Console’s URL Inspection distinguishes information about Google’s indexed version from a live test of whether a URL might be indexable. A live test does not establish that Google has already indexed the current page. [URL Inspection documentation](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289?hl=en).

Record the view and URL you checked. Do not change site-wide settings simply because one page needs attention.

### Investigate accidental restrictions carefully

A `noindex` instruction can tell Google not to include a page in search results. Google needs to crawl the page to see that instruction; blocking crawling through robots.txt is not an interchangeable way to apply it. [Google’s noindex guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/block-indexing).

Before removing a restriction, understand why it exists. Correct accidental settings on intended public pages without exposing material that should remain private.

- [ ] Identify the page’s intended search status.
- [ ] Compare the intent with the current setting.
- [ ] Record the proposed change and affected URLs.
- [ ] Verify the live result after implementation.

### Check discovery and the sitemap

Make sure important content is reachable through sensible navigation and relevant links. An article should not depend on the owner knowing its exact address.

A sitemap supplies information about pages and other files to search engines. Google explains that it can support discovery, but it does not guarantee crawling or indexing. [Google sitemap overview](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/overview).

Review whether the sitemap reflects the intended published pages. Investigate outdated or unexpected URLs rather than assuming every listed address should be indexed.

### Review loading and mobile experience

Test representative pages on a phone and a larger screen. Look for cropped content, unstable layouts, unreadable text, and controls that are difficult to use.

PageSpeed Insights separates laboratory diagnostics from field information based on real users when sufficient data exists. Check whether the result concerns the specific URL or a broader origin before interpreting it. [About PageSpeed Insights](https://developers.google.com/speed/docs/insights/v5/about).

Investigate the reported element or behavior before choosing a fix. A slow page does not automatically establish that the hosting subscription needs upgrading.

## On-page checks

### Review titles and headings together

Read the page title, main heading, introduction, and section headings as one outline. They should describe a coherent question or task.

A vague title such as “Our Solutions” may provide less useful direction than a specific service description. Rewrite for clarity rather than inserting more repetitions of a search phrase.

- [ ] The title describes the actual page.
- [ ] The main heading sets an expectation the content fulfills.
- [ ] The introduction answers or frames the central question.
- [ ] The sections follow a logical order.

Google’s starter guidance emphasizes clear, useful content and understandable page presentation. Use those principles rather than treating individual fields as a substitute for the whole page. [Google SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide).

### Check descriptions and internal links

Write page summaries that accurately describe the content. Do not use the same generic wording everywhere simply to fill a metadata field.

Follow contextual links and confirm that the destinations match the labels. A link promising a setup walkthrough should lead to the relevant instructions rather than an unrelated category page.

Check repeated components as well as article text. A wrong navigation destination can affect a much larger part of the site than one isolated link.

### Inspect images and supporting material

Check whether each image helps explain the page and displays appropriately on a narrow screen. Review relevant image descriptions and remove sample assets that do not represent the business.

Confirm that tables, downloads, and embedded resources are usable. If a document is restricted, a public webpage containing it may still leave visitors unable to access the material.

Keep the reader’s task central. A page can pass several automated checks while its most useful resource remains confusing or inaccessible.

## Content checks

### Confirm the page answers the right question

Summarize the reader’s task in one sentence, then compare the article with that task. If the headline promises a comparison, the page should actually explain the differences that affect a decision.

Review the current search results when the intended search task is unclear. A tutorial, product page, and working tool may serve different needs even when they mention similar words.

Use [our competitor keyword research guide](https://futureforwardstudios.com/competitor-keyword-research/) for structured discovery. The aim is understanding the audience, not reproducing another publisher’s article.

### Check facts, examples, and ownership

Review prices, product features, dates, contact information, and statements suggesting personal tests. Remove or correct claims without a reliable basis.

Label fictional examples clearly and use source links where they help readers assess changing information. Do not present generated copy as evidence of customer results or firsthand experience.

Google’s helpful-content guidance asks publishers to assess whether content supplies original value and leaves readers satisfied. Use that as an editorial review rather than trying to reach an arbitrary length. [People-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

### Review overlap before creating more pages

Identify pages that seem to answer the same central question. Decide whether they have distinct purposes or whether the useful material belongs together.

Do not delete a page solely because it receives little search traffic. Consider its value to customers, its supporting role, and any migration work before making a decision.

For a small site, a clearer existing guide may be more useful than another near-duplicate article. Record the reason for whichever action you choose.

### Test the next step

Follow the action the page encourages: read another guide, send an enquiry, download a resource, or continue a purchase. Confirm the destination works from the public version.

For a form, check receipt and the ability to respond. A visible success message is not the same as confirming that the business received the information.

## Prioritising fixes

### Rank verified problems by consequence

Start with intended public pages that cannot be accessed and essential customer actions that fail. Then investigate important indexing contradictions, repeated template issues, and content gaps.

Do not let a minor warning displace a broken enquiry route simply because the warning appears at the top of a report. Describe the consequence before assigning priority.

The following is an illustrative work queue, not the outcome of an audit of a real website.

| Finding | Why it matters | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Contact link leads to a missing page | Visitors cannot follow the intended enquiry route | Correct the link and test the published journey |
| Public guide has an unintended indexing restriction | Search settings conflict with the page’s purpose | Confirm intent, correct the setting, and recheck |
| Several articles have unclear titles | Readers struggle to identify their purposes | Review the template and affected page titles |
| One guide lacks a useful example | The explanation is less practical | Add a relevant, accurately labeled example |

### Create a usable SEO site audit report

For every issue, record the URL, observation, evidence, proposed action, owner, and completion check. Include the audit date and scope so the document remains understandable later.

Separate observations from explanations. A page being unavailable during a check does not, by itself, establish why search traffic changed.

Keep the action list short enough to manage. If a finding needs investigation, assign that investigation instead of presenting an unproven fix as settled work.

### Check dependencies before implementing

Some changes should happen in a particular order. If a service page is being replaced, decide the destination and navigation changes together rather than fixing one link and leaving the rest of the journey inconsistent.

Group related findings where that makes the work clearer. Several incorrect titles may share a publishing-template cause, while several broken links may require separate destination decisions.

Keep a recovery route for significant changes and identify who approves them. The audit report should support an orderly process rather than encourage hurried edits across the whole site.

Also distinguish the person doing the work from the person checking it. A second review can catch a corrected field that still creates confusing wording or a technical fix that disrupts an important action.

Close the item only when its completion check passes. If new evidence changes the proposed solution, update the record so future readers understand what was actually done.

### Verify and close the loop

After implementation, check the affected page and the relevant diagnostic again. If a shared template changed, inspect more than one page type and repeat the important visitor journeys.

Record what changed and whether the original problem was resolved. Retain unfinished questions rather than marking the whole site healthy because a score improved.

SEO audits are most useful when they produce understood actions and verified improvements. Review access, content, and the customer journey together, then prioritize the work that makes the website more useful and dependable.

## FAQ

### Can I perform an SEO audit myself?

Yes: begin with a defined set of pages, browser checks, and tools suited to specific questions. Ask for help when a finding requires technical changes you do not understand, and keep the evidence available for that person.

### How often should I audit my website?

Choose a review schedule that fits how often the site changes and how important its workflows are. Also review affected areas after substantial edits, migrations, or a verified problem rather than relying only on a calendar.

### Do I need paid software for an SEO audit?

A small audit can begin with manual review, Search Console, and free page diagnostics. Consider paid software when a repeated task or larger site scope justifies its capabilities and cost.

### Will fixing every audit warning improve my rankings?

No audit report can guarantee a ranking outcome, and some flagged conditions may be intentional. Verify the finding, understand its consequence, and make changes that fit the page’s purpose.
