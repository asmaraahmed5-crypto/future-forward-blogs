# How to Find the Keywords a Website Uses (3 Easy Methods)

To learn how to look up keywords on a website, inspect its page source, use relevant free tools, and try site-restricted searches. These methods reveal topic signals and research ideas; they do not automatically show every query the page ranks for or the traffic it receives.

## Why check a page's keywords

### Understand what the page helps people do

Checking webpage keywords can help you identify the subject, audience, and task behind a page. That is useful when reviewing your own content or researching what other publishers cover.

Start by reading the visible page. Look at the main heading, introduction, section headings, and the action the reader is encouraged to take.

Write a one-sentence summary before collecting phrases. A page about comparing hosting renewals serves a different task from a page explaining how to migrate a website, even though both discuss hosting.

### Separate three kinds of evidence

A phrase used in the content tells you what appears on the page. Metadata can suggest how the publisher describes it, while ranking reports concern search visibility.

Keep those categories separate in your notes. Neither a repeated phrase nor a well-written title proves that the page receives visitors from a particular search.

For broader research across several competitors, follow [our competitor keyword research guide](https://futureforwardstudios.com/competitor-keyword-research/). The methods here focus on understanding individual pages and locating relevant content.

## Method 1: page source — how to look up keywords on a website

### Step 1: open the public page

Choose a specific article or service page rather than beginning with an entire domain. Save its URL and note what the visible page appears to explain.

Use your browser’s page-source option where available. Browser menus differ, so look for a command such as View Page Source rather than assuming one shortcut works everywhere.

### Step 2: find the title and description

Search the source for `<title>` and for a meta element with `name="description"`. These can help you understand how the page has been labeled and summarized.

A simplified example might look like this:

```html
<title>How to Compare Hosting Renewal Costs</title>
<meta name="description" content="Compare the full cost of hosting before buying.">
```

This is an illustrative example, not code taken from a competitor. It suggests a cost-comparison topic without establishing any particular ranking.

Google uses several sources to generate title links and may display wording different from the supplied title. Do not assume the browser title and search-result title will always match. [Google title-link documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link).

### Step 3: inspect headings and visible content

Look for headings such as `<h1>` and `<h2>`, then compare them with the page you read. Note the phrases that describe its main task rather than listing every word in the HTML.

Source files can include navigation, repeated template text, scripts, and material unrelated to the main article. Do not mistake those elements for a carefully selected keyword list.

If you want to understand how to find keywords on a webpage, this comparison matters more than counting repeated words. The content’s purpose should remain clear when you read it normally.

### Step 4: use Inspect when needed

If source inspection does not reveal the text you can see, investigate the rendered page structure. In Chrome, right-clicking an element and choosing Inspect opens its location in the Elements panel. [Chrome’s DOM inspection guide](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/dom/).

Use this to check the heading or paragraph that interests you. You do not need to modify the code to understand its content.

Keep the research simple. The goal is identifying useful page information, not interpreting every script or configuration value.

### Ignore the meta keywords shortcut

You may find a field named `keywords` and assume it reveals a page’s SEO targets. Google states that it does not use the meta-keyword tag for indexing or ranking. [Google’s supported metadata guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/special-tags).

If the field exists, it records something the publisher entered. It is not a verified list of search queries bringing visitors to the page.

## Method 2: free tools

### Use Keyword Planner for topic suggestions

Google Keyword Planner includes a “Start with a website” option under keyword discovery. It looks for ideas related to the entered website’s content rather than reporting a confirmed list of its organic rankings. [Keyword Planner instructions](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243?hl=en).

The current setup instructions include account requirements, including billing information for basic idea-generation access. Review those requirements before proceeding and keep research separate from authorizing advertising activity.

Compare suggestions with the actual page. Remove unrelated subjects and do not assume every idea belongs in your own content plan.

### Use Search Console for a website you manage

For your own site, Search Console’s performance report shows search information including queries, pages, clicks, and impressions. This is a different type of evidence from reading a competitor’s source code. [Search Console performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553?hl=en).

Review the relevant page and the queries associated with it. Ask whether the content answers the intended task clearly or needs a better explanation.

Do not use this method to imply access to someone else’s private report. External tools may estimate competitor visibility, but that is separate from your own Search Console data.

### Choose tools by the question

Use a topic-generation tool for ideas, page inspection for visible and structural clues, and your own performance report for existing-site evidence. Each answers a different question.

For a wider shortlist, read [our free keyword tool comparison](https://futureforwardstudios.com/free-keyword-research-tools/) or [the free and paid research guide](https://futureforwardstudios.com/best-keyword-research-tools/).

Avoid collecting several reports that simply repeat similar suggestions. Record what each input changes about the next decision.

## Method 3: search operators

### Search within one domain

A query such as `site:example.com hosting` can help locate pages associated with a subject on a particular domain. Replace the example domain and topic with your research target.

Google documents `site:` as a way to restrict results to a domain, URL, or URL prefix. It also warns that the returned list is not necessarily exhaustive. [Google site-operator documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/search-operators/all-search-site).

Use this method when deciding how to search a website for keywords across several pages. It is useful for finding content to inspect, not measuring the complete archive.

### Narrow the question

Try a more specific topic, such as `site:example.com hosting renewal`, when the broad query produces unrelated material. Read the pages rather than treating the result snippets as the complete answer.

A narrower query can help you distinguish a buying guide from an installation tutorial. Save the relevant URLs with a short note about the task each one serves.

If a result is absent, do not assume the website has no relevant content. Check navigation and other available discovery routes before drawing that conclusion.

### Understand what the results cannot prove

A site-restricted search does not provide an authoritative ranking list for the domain. Google explains that a `site:` query without additional terms does not rank results in that way.

Likewise, the number of results is not a reliable inventory of every indexed page. Use the operator for discovery rather than turning it into an unsupported performance metric.

## Using what you find

### Create a small page-research record

Record the URL, title, main heading, central question, and useful supporting phrases. Add the research method and date so the observations remain understandable later.

Then write what the evidence establishes. “The page explains renewal costs” is a defensible observation; “this phrase generates most of its traffic” needs a different supporting source.

### Turn observations into a useful action

For your own page, check whether its title, introduction, and sections describe the same task. If they do not, clarify the page’s purpose before adding more phrases.

For competitor research, identify an audience question you can answer distinctly. Do not reproduce another publisher’s headings simply because they appear optimized.

Use related wording when it helps the explanation. There is no need to insert awkward search phrases or create separate pages for tiny variations of the same question.

### Work through an example without guessing traffic

Imagine a fictional page whose title is “Compare Hosting Renewal Costs.” Its main sections explain introductory offers, payment terms, and domain renewals.

Your research note could say that the page targets the reader’s cost-comparison task. Supporting phrases might include renewal rate, upfront payment, and domain registration, because those ideas appear in the explanation.

That note does not establish monthly search volume, ranking position, or revenue. Keep those fields blank unless you have an appropriate source, and identify estimates as estimates when you record them.

Now decide what your own page could add. A worked budget separating cash due today from future renewal charges could be useful if your intended audience needs that explanation.

The example shows how to move from visible content to an editorial idea without inventing the competitor’s performance. It also prevents source inspection from becoming a word-counting exercise with no practical outcome.

### Keep the checks proportionate

You do not need to inspect every tag on every page. Begin with the title, main heading, central task, and relevant sections, then use another method only when it answers a missing question.

Save a concise record and move to the decision. Research becomes more useful when it produces a clear improvement than when it simply grows into a larger collection of copied phrases.

### Decide whether a new page is needed

Review your own archive before adding another URL. One existing guide may already address the central need and simply require a clearer section or example.

For instance, [our website platform guide](https://futureforwardstudios.com/website-building-platforms/) serves a different purpose from [our business website setup walkthrough](https://futureforwardstudios.com/free-business-website/). Keep those distinct tasks in mind when grouping ideas.

Once you understand how to look up keywords on a website, the useful next step is improving the answer you provide. Combine page evidence, appropriate tool data, and editorial judgment instead of treating a list of words as a finished strategy.

## FAQ

### How can I see which keywords a website uses?

Read the visible page, inspect its title and headings, and use relevant research tools or site-restricted searches. Those methods reveal topic clues and suggestions rather than a complete confirmed ranking list.

### Can page source reveal all a website’s ranking keywords?

No: source code can show content and metadata, but it does not establish every search query associated with the page. For a site you manage, review its own search-performance report for the relevant evidence.

### Does the meta keywords tag help Google rankings?

Google states that the meta-keyword tag has no effect on its indexing or ranking. Do not treat that field as a shortcut to finding the queries that bring a website visitors.

### Can I search an entire website for a topic?

A `site:` query can help discover relevant pages within a domain, while a site’s own navigation or search may offer another route. Google warns that site-restricted results are not exhaustive, so absence from that query is not proof that a page does not exist.
